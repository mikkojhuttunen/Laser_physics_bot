### 1.1 Spontaneous and Stimulated Emission, Absorption ###

• Consider two energy levels, labelled with 1 and 2, of an atom or a molecule, with energies E₁ and E₂ (E₁ < E₂)

• Level 1 is the ground level, level 2 is the excited level

• Energy difference E₂ - E₁ is released by the atom, when it relaxes from the excited state, into the ground state

• Frequency ν₀ of the radiated wave is given by ν₀ = (E₂ - E₁)/(h), where h is the Planck’s constant

• Spontaneous emission of a photon of energy hν₀ = E₂ - E₁ causes the atom to decay from level 2 to level 1

• Radiative emission is one of the two possible decay mechanisms, the other one is non-radiative decay

• In non-radiative decay, the energy difference E₂ - E₁ (or part of it) is released in a form other than electromagnetic radiation (e.g. kinetic or internal energy of surrounding atoms or molecules)
⇒ In practise meaning that the material heats up

• There are three fundamental light-matter interactions in a two-level system:

• Absorption

• Stimulated emission

• Spontaneous emission

## Spontaneous Emission

• Let's start considering spontaneous emission

• In the case of spontaneous emission, the population of the excited states relaxes to the ground state

• We define A as the rate of spontaneous emission (also called Einstein's A coefficient), i.e., the fraction of population that actually decays to the ground state in an infinitesimal interval of time (dt), the population of the excited state evolves in time according to the following rate equation:

(d N₂)/( dt)|ₛₚ = - A N₂

• Note the minus sign, indicating that the population of the excited state decreases as a function of time due to relaxation to the ground state

• The solution to the above equation is therefore an exponentially decaying function of form

N₂(t) = N₂(0) e^(-t/τₛₚ) ,

• where we have introduced a so-called spontaneous emission lifetime (or radiative lifetime) τₛₚ = 1/A

• The actual value of τₛₚ is a property of the medium/system, and it depends on the particular energy transition considered

• The radiative lifetime τₛₚ can vary from picoseconds to some milliseconds, with typical values ranging from 10-100 ns

• The physical meaning of τₛₚ is that of the time required, for the population of the excited state, to have almost completely relaxed to the ground state

• In particular, at time t = τₛₚ, the upper-state population is

N₂ (t = τₛₚ) = N₂(0)/e ≃ 0.36 N₂(0)

• and therefore approximately 70% of the population of the excited state has decayed to the ground state

## Stimulated Emission

• Let's consider next the process of stimulated emission

• In the case of stimulated emission, the relaxation of the population of the excited state is triggered by an external electromagnetic field, that is interacting with the medium

• The population dynamics remains qualitatively the same, i.e.,

(d N₂)/( dt)|ₛₜ = - W₂₁ N₂

• where W₂₁ is the rate of stimulated emission

• Contrary to the previous cases, where the characteristic coefficient only depended on the properties of the medium, W₂₁ depends both on the medium (i.e., the transition energy between the excited and the ground state), and the intensity of the impinging electromagnetic field

• In fact, the more photons interact with the medium, the more photons are emitted via stimulated emission! This is quantified by writing the rate of stimulated emission as

W₂₁ = σ₂₁ F,

• where σ₂₁ is the stimulated emission cross-section (measured in m⁻²), and F is the photon flux (measured in number of photons per unit area, per unit time, i.e., s⁻¹m⁻²)

## Absorption

• Finally, we consider absorption

• Contrary to the processes described above, absorption affects the evolution of the population of the ground state, and it is, as stimulated emission, influenced by the presence of an external electromagnetic field

• In this case, we define the rate of absorption as W₁₂, so that we can write the corresponding rate equation as

(d N₁)/( dt)|_(abs) = - W₁₂ N₁

• As we discussed for the rate of stimulated emission, W₁₂ is proportional to the absorption cross-section σ₁₂ and the photon flux F:

W₁₂ = σ₁₂F

• Note that absorption and stimulated emission are, loosely speaking, inverse processes of each other

• This is no coincidence, but rather a fundamental property of atomic media, implying ultimately that the absorption and stimulated emission cross-sections are identical

• In other words, we have equality

σ₁₂ = σ₂₁ ≡ σ

• The proof of this identity, or equality, was made by Einstein, and will be the subject of the next Chapter

• In simple terms, the above identity means, that the probability for the active medium to absorb an incoming photon is identical to the probability for the same medium to emit a photon through stimulated emission

### 1.2 The Laser Idea ###

• Laser operation is in principle simple and can be divided into three steps

• Achieve light amplification in the active medium via stimulated emission

• Introduce a feedback mechanism to sustain amplification (i.e.~oscillation)

• Achieved using an optical cavity

• The optical cavity also introduces losses

• Supply energy to the medium via a pumping mechanism

• Needed to exceed lasing threshold and start the lasing

• Next we will briefly explain these functions and governing main principles

## Light Amplification

• The first key aspect of a laser is the amplification provided by the active medium

• Consider a photon flux F interacting with the active medium, across a beam cross-section S

• The change in the number of photons over time, is proportional to the change in photon flux due to the active medium

( # photons per unit time ) ∝ S dF

• Non-radiative decay processes and spontaneous emission do not affect F:

• Non-radiative decay does not emit photons^*
^*To be precise, non-radiative decay doesn't result in emission of photons into the lasing mode

• Spontaneous emission emits photons in random directions, contributing negligibly to the lasing beam

• Only absorption and stimulated emission contribute to dF:

S dF = ( (dN₁)/(dt) )_(abs) S dz + ( (dN₂)/(dt) )ₛₜᵢ S dz

• Since S dz = V (infinitesimal volume), we can use the rate equations to write:

dF = F N₂ σ₂₁ dz - F N₁ σ₁₂ dz = F (N₂ - N₁ ) σ dz ,~ σ₂₁ = σ₁₂ ≡ σ

• This simplifies to:

(dF)/(dz) = σ F ( N₂ - (g₂)/(g₁) N₁ ) ~,~ gₙ = the degeneracy of state n

• For amplification, the photon flux must increase, i.e., (dF)/(dz) > 0

• The condition for amplification becomes:

N₂ > (g₂)/(g₁) N₁

• If this condition is met:

• Stimulated emission dominates ⇒ medium acts as an amplifier

• If this condition is not met:

• Absorption dominates ⇒ medium acts as an absorber

• At thermal equilibrium, the ground state is more populated than the excited state, as it corresponds to the system's minimum energy

• Population distribution at equilibrium is governed by the Boltzmann distribution:

Nₙ ∝ gₙ e^(-Eₙ/k_B T)

where:

• Nₙ is the population of state n

• gₙ is the degeneracy of state n

• Eₙ is the energy of state n

• k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³~J/K is Boltzmann's constant

• T is temperature in kelvins

• The ratio of excited to ground state population becomes:

(N₂)/(N₁) ≈ (g₂)/(g₁) e^(-(E₂ - E₁)/k_B T)

• Since E₂ > E₁, it follows that (N₂)/(N₁) < (g₂)/(g₁), hence the amplification condition:

N₂ > (g₂)/(g₁) N₁

• is not satisfied at thermal equilibrium

• Therefore, any active medium in thermal equilibrium behaves as an absorber, not as an amplifier

• To achieve amplification, the system must be driven out of equilibrium via a pumping mechanism

• Example: Nd:YAG laser

• Energy gap: E₂ - E₁ = 1.165~eV

• Corresponding wavelength: λ = 1064~nm

• At T = 300~K (room temperature), we get:

(N₂)/(N₁) ≈ e^(-1.165 / (25 × 10^-3)) ≈ 10⁻²¹

• implying N₂ ≪ N₁ (negligible excited-state population)

• Even at very high temperatures (T → ∞), the best possible outcome is N₂ = N₁, which still does not satisfy the lasing condition

• Conclusion: Amplification is impossible at thermal equilibrium, regardless of temperature

## The Laser Condition

• Assume the active medium is out of equilibrium (via suitable pumping), such that amplification is possible

• To sustain amplification and achieve laser operation, a positive feedback mechanism is required

• This is implemented using a cavity resonator, typically composed of two mirrors

• Consider a photon flux F₀ at point P in the cavity. After one round trip, the flux becomes:

F = F₀ (1 - Lᵢₙₜ) G R₂ G (1 - Lᵢₙₜ) R₁

where:

~~Lᵢₙₜ = internal losses (scattering, absorption,...)

R₁,~R₂ = reflectivities of the two cavity mirrors

G = gain provided by the active medium

• The gain G is derived from integrating the photon flux equation:

F = F₀ e^(σ L (N₂ - (g₂)/(g₁) N₁ )) ≡ F₀ G,

• where L is the length of the active medium

• After a full round trip, the photon flux becomes

F = F₀ (1 - Lᵢₙₜ)² R₁ R₂ e^(2 σ L Δ N)

• where we have defined the population inversion parameter:

Δ N = N₂ - (g₂)/(g₁) N₁

• The term (1 - Lᵢₙₜ)² R₁ R₂ represents total cavity losses, and e^(2 σ L Δ N) is the round-trip gain

• For laser operation, the photon flux must increase with each round trip:

F ≥ F₀ ⇒ (1 - Lᵢₙₜ)² R₁ R₂ e^(2 σ L Δ N) ≥ 1

• Define the threshold (critical) population inversion Δ N_c such that:

Δ N ≥ Δ N_c ≡ ln(R₁) + ln(R₂) + 2 ln(1 - Lᵢₙₜ) 2 σ L

• When Δ N = Δ N_c, the gain exactly compensates for the cavity losses

• Once this threshold is met, laser oscillation can begin, even from a single spontaneously emitted photon

## Logarithmic Losses

• For the case of critical population inversion, Δ N_c can be simplified using the concept of logarithmic losses

• Define:

γ_(1,2) ≡ -ln(R_(1,2)) and γᵢ ≡ -ln(1 - Lᵢ)

• Then, total logarithmic loss is:

γ = γ₁ + γ₂ + 2 γᵢ

• The critical population inversion becomes:

Δ N_c = (γ)/(2σ L)

• In practice, Lᵢ ≪ 1, and similarly T₁, T₂ ≪ 1 (where T₁, T₂ are mirror transmittivities)

• Since R_(1,2) = 1 - T_(1,2), we can approximate logarithms using the Taylor expansion:

ln(1 - x) ≈ -x for x ≪ 1

• Applying this to the total logarithmic loss, we get:

γ ≃ T₁ + T₂ + 2 Lᵢ

• This first-order approximation simplifies practical calculations of the threshold condition for lasing

### 1.3 Pumping Schemes ###

## Population inversion

• Population inversion is a
necessary condition for lasing:

Nₑ > N_g

where Nₑ is the population of the excited state and N_g of the ground state

• A pumping mechanism is needed to achieve population
inversion, driving the system out of thermal equilibrium

## 2-Level System

• Population inversion cannot be achieved in a two-level system:

• Absorption and stimulated emission occur at equal rates when Nₑ = N_g

• Net change in photon flux becomes zero, thus no amplification occurs

• Even with continuous pumping, the system can only reach an equilibrium, but not inversion

• Therefore, two-level systems cannot support laser operation

• To enable population inversion and laser action, one must use a more complex system:

• Three-level or four-level atomic/molecular systems are typically employed

## 3-Level Systems

• Three-level system is the simplest extension beyond a two-level system

• In the 3-level system, pumping mechanism excites electrons from the ground state |g⟩ to an ancillary state |a⟩

• This is done using pump photons of frequency

νₚ = E₃ - E₁h

• being absorbed into the material, and exciting the system from the ground state to the ancillary state |a⟩

• System then undergoes a fast, non-radiative decay from |a⟩ to the upper laser level |e⟩

• Provided that the lifetime τ₃₂ of the state |a⟩ is short compared to the lifetime τ₂ of state |e⟩, population can accumulate in |e⟩

• Laser transition occurs between |e⟩ and |g⟩:

ν₀ = (E₂ - E₁)/(h)

• Population inversion between |e⟩ and |g⟩ becomes possible, allowing amplification of light at frequency ν₀

• Energy difference (also known as the Stokes shift) between |a⟩ and |e⟩ is known as the quantum defect

q = h ν_( p) - h ν_( 0) = h ν_( p) · ( 1- h ν_( 0)h ν_( p) )

• often given in percents, and is in practice an important parameter of lasers

• Larger quantum defect leads to faster heating of the gain medium

• Larger quantum defect also results in reduction of power efficiency

• Limitations of the three-level system:

• Requires a high-intensity pump to efficiently excite atoms from |g⟩ to |a⟩

• The ground state must be efficiently depleted, or population inversion will not be sustained

• Despite limitations, 3-level systems are historically important:

• The first working laser (ruby laser) was based on a three-level scheme

• Understanding their limitations motivates the need for improved systems, such as four-level systems or quasi-three-level system

## 4-Level Systems

• For four-level system a pump with frequency

νₚ = (E₃ - E_g)/h

correspond to the energy difference between the ground state |g⟩ and the highest energy level |3⟩

• System then relaxes (via non-radiative decay,
which is very fast) to the upper laser level |2⟩

• Population inversion can then be achieved between levels |2⟩ and |1⟩ and one can obtain amplification of an incoming flux with frequency ν₀ = (E₂ - E₁)/h, corresponding to the energy difference between the upper and the lower laser level

• The main difference (and advantage) with respect to a 3-level system is that, once in the lower laser level (|1⟩), the system very rapidly decays (via non-radiative decay) to the ground state (|g⟩)

• ⇒ In terms of population dynamics, the lower

laser lever is empty at all times (i.e.,~N₁ ≃ 0). Therefore, population inversion is easily achieved, as Δ N > 0 is always satisfied

• Four-level systems can therefore lead to very efficient amplification of light and lasing action

### 1.4 Properties of Laser Beams ###

## Monochromaticity

• Unique properties of laser light are monochromaticity, coherence, directionality and brightness

• Light emitted by a laser consists of a "single" frequency (or wavelength)

• This is due to the fact, that only light with that frequency exactly matching the laser transition (namely, for our 2-level system, ν₀ = (E₂ - E₁) /h) is amplified, while light of other frequencies cannot interfere with the active medium (or, more accurately, with the laser transition)

• In real life, no emission is purely monochromatic, but always spans a range of frequencies around the "nominal" central frequency ν₀

• This range is called linewidth^*
^*Full width at half maximum (FWHM) value of the peak is often used for linewidth

• Narrower the linewidth, the more

monochromatic the laser is

• An important factor contributing to the quasi-monochromaticity of laser light is the resonant cavity, which only allows laser oscillations (and, therefore, light emission) at the cavity resonant frequencies

## Coherence

• Coherence refers to the correlation in the electric field between two different points on the wavefront of a field

• If the two points are spatially separated on the wavefront, it is called spatial coherence (or transverse coherence)

• If the two points are the same location on the wavefront but evaluated at different times, we speak of temporal coherence (or longitudinal coherence)

## Spatial Coherence

• Spatial coherence is essential for performing interference experiments (with lasers)

• Consider two points P₁ and P₂ on the wavefront of an electric field

• If the phase difference between E(P₁) and E(P₂) remains constant over all points P on the wavefront, the field is said to have perfect spatial coherence

## Temporal Coherence

• Temporal coherence refers to the correlation in the electric field at two different times at the same point on the wavefront

• If the phase difference between E(t) and E(t + τ_c) remains constant for all 0 < t < τ_c , the beam is said to be temporally coherent over time τ_c , where

τ_c is called the coherence time

• If phase correlation holds for all values of τ_c , even letting τ_c tend to infinity, the field has perfect temporal coherence

• In practice, coherence time τ_c is finite, due to the laser’s non-perfect monochromaticity, quantitatively described using uncertainty relation

τ_c Δ ν 1

between τ_c and the laser linewidth Δ ν

• Representative values:

• Wavelength-tunable CW Ti:Sapphire, Δ ν ∼ 1~GHz, τ_c 1 ns

• Narrow-linewidth solid-state laser, Δ ν ∼ 1~kHz, τ_c 1 ms

• Narrow-linewidth fiber lasers, Δ ν ∼ 10-100~Hz, τ_c 10-100 ms

• Ultranarrow linewidth photonic-atomic lasers, Δ ν ∼ 1~Hz, τ_c 1 s (∼300 000 km coherence length!)

## Directionality

• Directionality of a laser beam derives directly from the fact, that lasing occurs inside an optical cavity

• Laser emission has high spatial coherence, and single transverse mode emission can be realized via cavity design
⇒ Beam divergence (quantified by divergence angle θ) of a single transverse mode laser can be made very small

• Examples of directionality:

• Already simple laser pointers have very small beam divergence (1 mrad) compared to incoherent sources ( 20 mrad, flashlights, LEDs)

• Lasers used in Lunar Laser Ranging with 0.5-5 m beam sizes have beam divergences around 5 μrad

• As a result, the radiation pattern that leaves the cavity, i.e.,~the laser beam, will propagate mainly along one particular direction (opposed, for example, to spherical waves, that do not have a well-defined propagation direction)

• Exhibits very small diffraction (i.e., the transverse beam size will grow very slowly along the propagation direction)

• Consider a spatially perfectly coherent plane wave diffracted by an aperture of finite size D

• Diffraction causes the wave to broaden in its transverse dimension in the far field

• This also applies to laser beams — due to their finite transverse size, they inevitably undergo diffraction^*^*position and momentums are canonical conjugate variables

• The divergence angle θ of a laser beam is approximately:

θ = (λ)/(π w₀),

where λ is the wavelength and w₀ is the beam waist

• This gives the minimum possible divergence, known as the diffraction limit

• If w₀ ≫ λ , then θ ≪ 1 , and the transverse size at a distance L (far field) becomes:

w_(0,f)(L) ≈ θ L,

indicating negligible broadening

• For a partially coherent beam, the divergence angle is modified to:

θ ≈ (λ)/(π δ_c),

• where δ_c is the spatial coherence radius

• We see that spatial coherence plays an important role in the directionality of laser beams

## Brightness

• Final important property of lasers are that they can be very bright light sources

• Brightness is defined as the power emitted by the source per unit surface area per unit solid angle

• Let's consider the surface element dS, and let us assume that radiation is emitted into a solid angle dΩ around the direction z

• The power dP emitted along that direction can then be written as

dP = B cosθ dS dΩ,

• where B is the brightness of the source emitting electromagnetic radiation

• If we assume, that the source is Lambertian (i.e., it emits isotropically), and recall, that for a laser beam we can approximate cosθ ≃ 1, and dΩ ≃ π θ²

• We then obtain

B = (P)/(π S θ²),

• where S = π w₀² is transverse area of the beam, and θ = (λ)/(π w₀)

• Using above equations, the brightness can simply be written as

B = (P)/(λ²) , which importantly doesn't depend on beam size!

### 2.1 Time-dependent Perturbation Theory ###

• Next we develop the necessary mathematical framework to calculate atomic cross sections, using a few assumptions

• Consider the simplest atomic model, i.e.~2-level system

• We start by considering the Schrödinger equation, for a system perturbed by an electromagnetic interaction:

i ℏ (∂)/(∂ t) |ψ(r, t)⟩ = H |ψ(r, t)⟩

where H is the Hamiltonian operator and |ψ(r, t)⟩ is the state vector representing the system under analysis

• If the system does not interact with anything, the Hamiltonian operator is time-independent, and represents the system’s total energy (kinetic + potential):

H ≡ H₀ = T + V

## Full Hamiltonian

• When the system interacts with an external perturbation (e.g., an electromagnetic field), the Hamiltonian must be modified into

H = H₀ + Hᵢₙₜ ,

where H₀ is the atomic Hamiltonian and Hᵢₙₜ accounts for the interaction with the electromagnetic field

• Under electric-dipole approximation Hᵢₙₜ is given by:

Hᵢₙₜ = E(r, t) · μ⃗

where E is the incident field and μ⃗ the electric dipole moment of the atom (μ⃗=-e x⃗)

## Atomic Hamiltonian

• Considering no incident electromagnetic field, we can use the time-independent Schrödinger equation to calculate the eigenstates of the system, solving the eigenvalue problem

H₀ | k ⟩ = Eₖ | k ⟩ , ~where~k = g, e

• Eigenstates are described by full time-dependent wavefunctions

Ψₖ(x, t) = φₖ(x) e^(-i Eₖ t / ℏ) ,

resulting in probability densities of

| Ψₖ(x, t) | ² = |φₖ(x) |² ,

which are independent of time, explaining why such states are called stationary

• Note, if the system is in one of its stationary eigenstates

(∂)/(∂ t) ⟨ k | A | k ⟩ ≡ (∂)/(∂ t) ⟩ = 0

applies for any operator A

• Stationary states also form a complete basis to represent the wavefunction (i.e., the state of the system) at any time

| ψ(r, t)⟩ = ∑ₖ Cₖ exp ( - (i Eₖ t)/(ℏ) ) | k ⟩

where the coefficients Cₖ are expansion coefficients, and thus |Cₖ |²
gives the probability of the system occupying eigenstate k, normalized such that ∑ₖ |Cₖ|² = 1

## Interaction Hamiltonian

• For a 2-level system this means:

|ψ(r, t)⟩ = C_g exp ( - (i E_g t)/(ℏ) ) |g⟩ + Cₑ exp ( - (i Eₑ t)/(ℏ) ) | e ⟩

where we define the resonance frequency of the atom as

ω₀ ≡ (Eₑ-E_g)/(ℏ)

• Now that we have a way to represent the wavefunction, we need a model for the interaction:

• Dipole interaction: Hᵢₙₜ= μ · E⃗(r, t)

• Classical electromagnetic field

• x-polarized (without loss of generality)

## Selection Rules

• For conceptual simplicity, we consider an incident monochromatic plane wave of form

E(r, t) = x E₀ cos (k z-ω t)

• NOTE:

• atomic dimensions are very small compared to wavelength λ of incident light (Bohr radius a_B ∼ 10⁻¹¹ m, λ ≫ a_B)
⇒ The atom sees a locally constant field!

• Electric dipole (moment) has odd parity (changes sign under inversion symmetry)

• Changing displacement vector rₖ → -rₖ changes the sign of the dipole moment μ⃗ → -μ⃗

•

• Since H_( int) ∝ μ, also the interaction Hamiltonian has odd parity and can therefore only connect atomic states with different parity

• This means in practise that for the ground state we have

⟨ g | H_(int) | g ⟩ = ⟨ X_(gg) ⟩ = 0 , ⟨ i | H_(int) | j ⟩≡ ⟨ Xᵢⱼ ⟩

written equivalently, using the integral notation, as

∫ g^*(r) H_( int) g(r) dr³ = ∫ even × even × odd = ∫ even × odd = 0

because interaction Hamiltonian is odd, while the ground state g(r) is even, resulting in even function (even x even = even)

•

• Similarly, wavefunction of the excited state e(r) would result in vanishing integral

⟨ e | H_(int) | e ⟩ = ⟨ Xₑₑ ⟩ =0

because excited states have an alternating parity, and multiplication of two odd functions is an even function (odd x odd = even)

• This can also be understood by saying that the state that we consider has no permanent dipole moment
⇒ Prompting us into off-topic thoughts whether there could be systems that would have a permanent dipole moment, analogous to ferromagnetic materials exhibiting permanent magnetic dipole moments

## Atomic + Interaction = Full

• We now put everything together to study the electron dynamics of a 2-level system:

i ℏ (∂)/(∂ t) | ψ(r, t)⟩ = [ H₀ + Hᵢₙₜ(t) ] | ψ(r, t) ⟩

where

H_( int)(t) = - E₀ d · x cos (ω t)

and

|ψ(r, t)⟩ = C_g exp ( - (i E_g t)/(ℏ) ) |g⟩ + Cₑ exp ( - (i Eₑ t)/(ℏ) ) | e ⟩

• We let the frequency ω of the incident field to be close to the transition frequency ω₀ between electron states, i.e.~that ω ≈ ω₀ ≡ (Eₑ-E_g)/ℏ

.#1

## Rabi Frequency

• First we assume to be working in the so-called interaction picture, allowing to forget the dynamics imposed to the system by the unperturbed Hamiltonian H₀

• Then we substitute the last two equations into the first, and project onto < g | and < e | to simplify the math (using ⟨ g | H_( int) | g ⟩=0 and ⟨ e | H_( int) | e ⟩=0), resulting in a

system of coupled ordinary differential equations:

❴ ~
i C_g(t) = Ω cos (ω t) e^(-i ω₀ t) Cₑ(t) ,

i Cₑ(t) = Ω cos (ω t) e^(i ω₀ t) C_g(t) ,

Ω = E₀ ⟨ X_(eg) ⟩ = E₀ ⟨ e | Hᵢₙₜ | g⟩

• where Ω is known as the Rabi frequency

## Power-series Expansion

• There are many ways to solve the above coupled system

• The second-easiest^*
is via power-series expansion of the C coefficients with respect to Ω (which functions as an interaction parameter)
^*Easiest option would be to just use a smart ansatz, showing via brute force calculation that it works

• Let's assume the electron is initially in the ground state, i.e., we have initial conditions C_g(0)=1, Cₑ(0)=0, and assume

C_g(t)= ∑ₙ₌₀^(∞) C_g⁽ⁿ⁾(t) Ωⁿ = C_g⁽⁰⁾(t) + C_g⁽¹⁾(t) Ω + C_g⁽²⁾(t) Ω² + ...

Cₑ(t) = ∑ₙ₌₀^(∞) Cₑ⁽ⁿ⁾(t) Ωⁿ = Cₑ⁽⁰⁾(t) + Cₑ⁽¹⁾(t) Ω + Cₑ⁽²⁾(t) Ω² + ...

• 1) Substitute this ansatz into the above equations

• 2) Order the terms for matching orders of Ω, and solve these newly formed coupled systems by starting from lowest order n=1 (n=0 case follows trivially from initial conditions: C_g⁽⁰⁾=1, Cₑ⁽⁰⁾=0)

• 3) Use the lower-order solutions to solve higher-order terms

• 4) Stop when enough orders have been solved

• EXAMPLE: up to first order, Ω¹

i [C_g ⁽¹⁾(t) Ω ] = Ω cos (ω t) e^(-i ω₀ t) [0+ Cₑ⁽¹⁾(t) Ω ] = O (Ω² ~or~higher) =0

i [Cₑ ⁽¹⁾(t) Ω ] = Ω cos (ω t) e^(-i ω₁ t) [C_g⁽⁰⁾(t) + C_g⁽¹⁾(t) Ω ]

= Ω cos (ω t) e^(-i ω₀ t)+ O( Ω²) = Ω cos (ω t) e^(-i ω₀ t)

## First-order Solution

• So we have

❴

C_g ⁽¹⁾(t) =0

Cₑ ⁽¹⁾(t) = cos (ω t) e^(i ω₀ t)

• which is straightforward to solve via integration

• This first-order solution gives us tools to calculate transition amplitudes, higher-order contributions would give rise to energy level shifts (Bloch--Siegert shift) and multi-photon processes not crucial for our understanding of lasers

• Continuing the expansion, you would notice a pattern:

a so-called rotating-wave approximation (RWA) holds

• We can next calculate the absorption cross-section for a 2-level system

• Next we move to consider multi-atom systems

### 2.5 Line-Broadening Mechanisms ###

• Let's discuss next what effects affect the lineshapes and linewidths of atomic transitions

• While proper distinctions between different types of lasers and lasing media are beyond our course, we note that there are other resonances than just atomic transitions^*^*Recall e.g.~quantum dots and quantum wells in semiconductors.

• If already familiar with basics of solid-state physics, it's also worth recalling the band structure theory of solids

• We can distinguish between two main different types of broadening mechanisms:

• Homogeneous broadening

• Inhomogeneous broadening

## Homogeneous Broadening

• We speak of homogeneous broadening, when all the atoms forming the active medium contribute identically

• All atoms possess the same lineshape function gₕ(ω - ω₀) centered over the same transition frequency ω₀

• Then:

σₕ(ω) = σₐtom(ω) = (π |μ|²)/(3 ε₀ cn ℏ ) ω₀ gₕ(ω - ω₀) ,

gₕ(ω - ω₀) = ( (4)/(Δ ω₀) ) (1)/(1+ ((2)/(Δ ω₀)(ω-ω₀) )²) ~~(Lorentzian lineshape)

• Δω₀ = 1 / τ_c, where τ_c is characteristic time, determined by specific process

• What creates homogeneous broadening?

• 1) Natural broadening: Homogeneous broadening results from the finite lifetime of the excited state of an atom, due to spontaneous emission

• If Nₑ(t) decays with characteristic time τₛₚ, then

Nₑ(t) = Nₑ(0)e^(-t/τₛₚ)

• The electric field associated to the spontaneous emission of a photon is

E(t) = E(0) e^(-t/τₛₚ+iω₀ t)

• and the spectrum of emitted light is then its Fourier transform

FT ❴E(t)❵ = gₕ(ω - ω₀)

• 2) Collision broadening: atoms collide with each other (in gases or liquids) or with other (quasi)particles --- such as with lattice vibrations, also known as phonons, in solids

• Collisions induce random phase jumps in the atomic dipole (Markovian approx.^*) with a characteristic time τ_(col)^*System's behavior depends only on the current state, not of its past.

• This causes the atom to interact coherently with the electromagnetic field only within this time, which is the average time between two consecutive collisions

• 3) Non-radiative decay: If there are non-radiative decay channels active in the atom, they can also induce homogeneous broadening, with a characteristic time τₙᵣ

• In general, the characteristic time of homogeneous broadening, or, equivalently the FWHM of the lineshape function, is determined by the sum of the inverse lifetimes of all these contributions:

Δω₀ = 1τₜₒₜ = 1τₛₚ + 1τ_( col) + 1τₙᵣ

## Inhomogeneous Broadening

• What creates inhomogeneous broadening?

• Atoms contribute differently to the overall absorption/emission of light

• The transition frequencies of the atoms that form the active medium vary from atom to atom, such that the transition frequencies ω₀' are distributed around a mean value ω₀ with a distribution g^*(ω₀'-ω₀), which is the inhomogeneous lineshape function

• However, each atom still exhibits homogeneous broadening (e.g.~spontaneous emission is always present!)

• Therefore, in the presence of inhomogeneous broadening, both effects must be considered:

gₜₒₜ(ω - ω₀) = ∫₀^∞ g^*(Ω) gₕ(ω - ω₀ - Ω) dΩ

• Note that if one of the two mechanisms dominates over the other one, then the block lineshape in practise takes the form of the dominating mechanism

• Let's consider an example of inhomogeneous broadening, assuming that Δ ωᵢₙₕ ≫ Δ ωₕₒₘ, so that

gₕ(ω - ω₀ - Ω) ≈ δ(ω - ω₀ - Ω)

• then

gₜₒₜ(ω - ω₀) = ∫₀^∞ g^(*)(Ω) δ(ω - ω₀ - Ω) dΩ = g^(*)(ω - ω₀)

• Looking at above equation, we see that the inhomogeneous broadening clearly dominates the formation of the lineshape and the linewidth

## Local-field Effects

• What creates inhomogeneous broadening in practise?

• 1) Inhomogeneity in the local electric field: for solids and liquids (recall local-field effects in crystalline solids)

• 2) Doppler effect: particularly relevant for gases

• Local-field effects are very important in practical solid-state lasers, e.g.~the broad tuning range of titanium--sapphire lasers (Ti:Sapphs, 700--1100 nm) relies on unique local-field effects affecting the properties of titanium atoms, residing inside the sapphire (α-Al₂O₃) host medium
⇒ Amorphous Al₂O₃ (alumina) doesn't the exhibit same properties than what crystalline sapphire does

## Doppler Broadening

• Doppler broadening takes place in a gaseous gain media, due to the velocity distribution of atoms/molecules (recall the Maxwell--Boltzmann speed distribution)

• Let's consider an electromagnetic field propagating in a gas composed of atoms moving at velocity v

• The Doppler effect shifts the resonance frequency of the moving particle

ω' = ω (1- (v)/(c) )

• Incident light oscillating at ω is absorbed only if ω' = ω₀ :

ω = (ω₀)/(1 - (v)/(c)) , where v varies between particles

• Thus, it can be shown that above gives rise to a Gaussian lineshape function

g^*(ω₀' - ω₀) = (2)/(Δ ω₀^*) ( (ln 2)/(π) )^(1/2) exp (- (4(ω₀' - ω₀)²)/((Δ ω₀^*)²) ln 2 )

Δ ω₀^* = 2ω₀ ( (2k_B T ln 2)/(Mc²) )^(1/2)

• where M is the mass of the atom, k_B the Boltzmann constant, and ln 2 is due to Δ ω₀^* being defined via the FWHM (and not by the 1/e) value

### 2.6 Saturation and Gain ###

## Absorption and Gain

• We start by assuming that steady-state condition is reached, meaning that populations N_g, and Nₑ are time independent

• Let's then consider how incident photon flux F affects the absorption:

dF_(abs) = - σ N_g F dz

• where minus sign signifies absorption (= loss for the field)

• Similarly for stimulated emission

dFₛₜ = + σ Nₑ F dz

• where plus sign signifies emission (= gain for the field)

• The change of the total photon flux is then the sum of these two processes:

dF = dF_(abs) + dFₛₜ = - σ (N_g - Nₑ) F dz

• which is readily integrated to yield

F(z) = F(0) e^(-σ(Nₑ-N_g)z)

• Let's next consider two different (steady-state) scenarios

• Case 1: N_g > Nₑ applies for state populations, resulting in

F(z) < F(0)

so that it makes sense to define

α(ω) ≡ σ(ω)(N_g - Nₑ) ⇒ Absorption coefficient

• Case 2: We have reached population inversion, Nₑ > N_g, resulting in

F(z) > F(0)

prompting to define

α(ω) ≡ σ(ω)(Nₑ - N_g) ⇒ Gain coefficient

• Since in general σ(ω) ∼ g(ω-ω₀), absorption and gain coefficients of the medium also depend on frequency

• In addition, gain coefficients depend on the broadening mechanism that is predominant in the medium

## Population Dynamics and Saturation

• Let's next look at the actual population dynamics, and not just the steady state

• Start from the case of absorption dominating, assuming that:

• Pumping optical field is strong (F ≫ 1)

• Upper-state population dominates, N_g ≫ Nₑ, resulting in absorption

• Proceed writing down the rate equations for the system:

❴

(dNₑ)/(dt) = - W (Nₑ - N_g ) - (Nₑ)/(τ)

(dN_g)/(dt) = W (Nₑ - N_g )

• where effective lifetime τ accounts for both spontaneous and non-radiative decay

• We introduce a parameter Δ N = Nₑ - N_g , known as population inversion, which is the parameter of interest here, and write a rate equation for its behavior:

(d(Δ N))/(dt) = (d Nₑ)/(dt) - (d N_g)/(dt)

• Using the fact that the total population is constant

Nₜₒₜ = N_g + Nₑ ,

• we can write:

(d(Δ N))/(dt) = d ( Nₜₒₜ -Nₑ ) dt - (dNₑ)/(dt) = -2 W Δ N + (2Nₑ)/(τ)

= -2 W Δ N + Nₜₒₜ-Δ Nτ

• Under the steady-state condition

(d(Δ N))/(dt) = 0

• we get

Δ N = Nₜₒₜ1 + 2Wτ

• Now, recalling that W = σ F and F = (I)/(ℏ ω) , we can rewrite the above expressions as:

Δ N = Nₜₒₜ1 + I/Iₛat , where Iₛₐₜ = (ℏ ω)/(2 σ τ)

• showing that the saturation intensity Iₛₐₜ is a property of the medium, being inversely proportional on the σ τ term

## Saturation of Absorption

• The absorption coefficient is now given by α = σ Δ N , i.e.,

α(ω) = σ(ω) Nₜₒₜ1 + I/Iₛₐₜ = α₀(ω) 1 + I/Iₛₐₜ , α₀(ω) = under weak pumping

• Absorption coefficient is inversely proportional to incoming intensity (giving rise to saturation effects)

• At strong pump intensities ( I > Iₛₐₜ ) we have

Δ N ≈ 0 ⇔ N_g ≈ Nₑ ⇒ α ≈ 0

• giving rise to induced transparency, i.e. vanishing of absorption

• Note that saturation of absorption becomes visible already at lower intensities I < Iₛₐₜ

• Looking at above math, we see that saturation intensity corresponds to the intensity of the pump for which the absorption coefficient decreases by a factor of 2

• Next we start generalizing our treatment, by asking how does possible inhomogeneity of the medium affect absorption?

• Recall first, that for the case of homogeneous broadening, saturation occurs identically for each atom
⇒ Homogeneous line shape is preserved
⇒ The absorption strength is uniformly reduced at different wavelengths

## Spectral Hole Burning

• The situation is very different if the absorption line shape is inhomogeneously broadened

• Each atom contributes differently to the envelope

• Each atom has a different Iₛat (since ω₀' varies per objects)

• Only those absorbing objects with ω=ω₀' will saturate, while the other ones do not
⇒ Gives rise to spectral hole burning

## Gain Saturation

• Let's now consider what happens in the case of gain, considering a 4-level system for conceptual clarity

• Population inversion must hold:

Δ N = N₂ - N₁ > 0

• Need to look at the rate of change of N₂ , but it is easier to look at Δ N , since in a 4-level system the level | 1⟩ is practically always empty, so that Δ N ∼ N₂

• We therefore have:

(d Δ N)/(dt) ≃ (dN₂)/(dt) = Rₚ - W Δ N - (N₂)/(τ) where Rₚ = pumping rate

• At steady state (Δ N is constant):

Rₚ - W Δ N - (Δ N)/(τ) = 0
⇒
Δ N = (Rₚ τ )/(1 + W τ)

• We can write this analogously to the earlier treatment for absorption, by introducing the intensity through

W = σ F ⇒ W = σ I/ ℏ ω

• resulting in

W τ = σ τ (I)/(ℏ ω) ≡ I/Iₛat ⇒ Iₛat = (ℏ ω )/(σ τ)

• So that

Δ N = Rₚ τ1 + I/Iₛat ⇒ g(ω) = σ(ω) Δ N = σ (ω) Rₚ τ1 + I/Iₛat = g₀1 + I/Iₛat

• We note that

I⁽ᵍᵃⁱⁿ⁾ = 2 I⁽ᵃᵇˢ⁾

• so gain also saturates, at higher intensities than absorption

• Characteristic parameters here are the unsaturated gain g₀ (gain under no incident pump), and the saturation intensity Iₛₐₜ, dictated by the properties of the medium

• Let's next study how the gain is influenced by the different line broadening mechanisms:

• For purely homogeneously broadened medium:

• If I/Iₛₐₜ = 1 ⇒ gain drops from g₀ to g₀/2

• If I/Iₛₐₜ ≫ 1 ⇒ gain visibly decreases due to saturation

• When inhomogeneous broadening takes also place:

• Spectral hole burning starts taking place

• Lasing frequencies might experience a too low gain to overcome the lasing threshold and then contribute to the laser action

• Other frequencies can still experience a relatively strong gain, this can enable building up of noise, via amplified spontaneous emission (ASE)

### 3.2 Fabry–Pérot Interferometer ###

• Fabry-Pérot (FP) interferometers (or resonators) are optical cavities formed by two planar partially reflecting mirrors, placed parallel with each other

• The formed FP cavity is resonant at specific wavelengths, trapping light inside the resonator for several multiple reflections

• Such components are used in numerous photonic applications, ranging from short laser cavities (e.g. so-called vertical cavity surface emitting lasers (VCSELs)), spectral frequency filters (e.g. as etalons inside laser cavities), or in spectral analysis applications in general

rᵢ² + tᵢ² = 1

• where a lossless system has been assumed, conserving energy

E_(1 t)= t^() t E₀ e^(i φ) , φ = propagation phase from A to B

E_(2 t)= (r^() )² t^() t E₀ e^(i φ) e^(i δ) , δ= (4 π)/(λ) nₜ d cos θₜ

• A repeating factor is given by (r^())² e^(i δ) and the total transmitted field then becomes

Eₜ= t t^() E₀ e^(i φ) [1 + (r^())² e^(i δ)+… ] ≈ t t^() E₀ e^(i φ)1-(r^())² e^(i δ)= (1-r²) E₀ e^(i φ)1-r² e^(i δ)

• where we have taken use of the geometric series

∑ₖ₌₁ⁿ a rᵏ⁻¹= (a (1-rⁿ ))/(1-r), when r ≠ 1

## Transmission Function

• A transmission coefficient (operating on irradiances rather than on fields) is commonly defined as

T = | EₒᵤₜEᵢₙ |²
= (1)/(1 + (4R)/((1 - R)²) sin² ( (δ φ)/(2) )) = A

• giving the transmissivity of a Fabry-Pérot Interferometer, which is also called the Airy function

• Implicit assumptions in above are:

• r = r' , resulting in R = |r|² = |r'|²

• δ φ = (2π ν)/(c) Λ, where Λ =
2 nₜ d cos θₜ, resulting in wavelength-/frequency-dependent transmissivity, i.e. T = T(ν)

## Free Spectral Range

• Airy function ( T(ν) ) is a periodic function (series of pulses) with a period:

Δ ν_(FSR) = (c)/(2n L) = (c)/(2nd cos θ)

• where L is the cavity length, and Δ ν_(FSR) is known as the free spec- tral range (FSR)

• Free spectral range can be described directly by sin² (δφ/2) since this is the function that makes T(ν) periodic

## Finesse

• The transmission peaks have a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) value of Δ ν_c , which needs to be determined (not an easy task without making many assumptions)

• Peaks have Lorentzian lineshapes and correspond to frequencies for which there is constructive interference between the various output fields

• Let's calculate the FWHM:

T(ν_c) = (1)/(2) ⇒ (1)/(1 + (4 R)/((1 - R)²) sin² ( (δ φ_c)/(2) )) = (1)/(2)
, δ φ_c = (4π ν_c)/(c) n d

• assuming normal incidence for simplicity, then replacing d with dcos θ

• Considering the term

(4 R)/((1 - R)²) sin²( (δ φ_c)/(2) ) = 1 ⇒ sin² ( (δ φ_c)/(2) ) = ((1-R)²)/(4R) ≈ 0 for R≈ 1

• Taylor expanding up to first order gives

sin²( (δ φ_c)/(2) ) ≈ ( (δ φ_c)/(2) )² ⇒ (δ φ_c)² ≈ ((1-R)²)/(R) ⇒ δ φ_c ≈ ± (1-R)/R^(1/2)

• Taylor expand up to first order in δ φ to get:

δ φ_c = 2 π ν_cΔ ν_(F S R) ⇒ Δ ν_c = ν_c^+ - ν_c ^- = 2 ν_c = Δ ν_(FSR) 1 - Rπ R^(1/2)

• giving us the FWHM value for Fabry-Perot transmission peaks

• Using above, we can define an important parameter for a Fabry-Pérot interferometer, known as finesse:

F = Δ ν_(FSR)Δ ν_c = π R^(1/2)1 - R

• Finesse corresponds to the quality factor for the FP interferometer

• Measures the ability of the interferometer to store energy

• Higher the finesse, the more times light bounces back and forth before being significantly attenuated

• Dictated by the reflectivities of input/output surfaces

### 3.3 Optical Resonators ###

## Fabry–Pérot Resonator

• Next we study optical resonators, in the simplest case consisting of two (planar) mirrors (forming a Fabry-Pérot interferometer), between which light bounces multiple times

• As we learned in our previous chapter, for specific incident wavelengths, propagating light interferes constructively/destructively, giving rise to resonances

• These resonances occur when the accumulated phase of propagating light during a single round trip corresponds to a multiple of 2π (i.e.~we have periodic boundary conditions)

• The total field inside the cavity resembles a stationary wave

• Mathematically, the phase condition corresponds to

k 2L = 2π n = (2π ν)/(c) 2L , here n=integer

• Resonant frequencies of the cavity are therefore

ν = n (c)/(2L)

• with the free spectral range (FSR, the separation between adjacent resonance peaks) given by

Δ ν_(FSR) = (c)/(2L)

## Ring Resonator

• There are also cavity configurations where light doesn't propagate bidirectionally, i.e. doesn't form standing waves

• These are called unidirectional, or travelling-wave cavities

• Ring resonators are prime examples of this kind of travelling-wave cavities

• Constructive interference after one round trip is now

kL = 2π n

• Which results in resonant frequencies ν, and FSR given by

ν = n · (c)/(L) , Δ ν_(FSR) = (c)/(L)

## Concentric Resonator

• As we learn during our next lecture, cavities formed by two planar mirrors are only marginally stable, making their use as laser resonators very unpractical

• Stable cavities can be formed by using one or two curved mirrors

• In general one can use two unidentical spherical mirrors, with centers of curvatures given by C₁ and C₂

• Simplest is formed when mirrors are identical, and is called a concentric resonator

• The points C₁ = C₂≡ C then lie at the center of the resonator

• Constructive interference after one round trip results in

❴

k 2L = 2 π n,

L = 2R

• giving resonant frequencies and FSR as

ν = (c)/(4R)n , Δ ν_(FSR) = (c)/(4R)

• To conclude, the resonance frequencies and the FSR of all cavities follows similar math, dictated by the cavity length and by the periodic boundary conditions imposed by the mirrors

• Main difference we currently see between different cavity lengths, is that the FSR changes accordingly

### 3.4 Stability Condition ###

• Modes, or eigenmodes are a fundamental concept in physics in general, being relevant to all problems/situations that can be cast into an eigenvalue problem

• Here, we are interested to understand the limitations imposed to the transverse and longitudinal profiles of the fields due to boundary conditions

• Resonant frequencies ⇒ define the longitudinal modes of the field inside the cavity

• Transverse eigenmodes of the cavity:

• Solutions of the paraxial equation

• Exhibit the same symmetries as the cavity

• We seek for a shape-preserving mode = eigenmode

• Constraint: After one round-trip (cavity length of L), the transverse profile of the electric field repeats itself (i.e., it is self-similar)

u(x, y, 0) = u(x, y, 2L) = σ u(x,y,0) e^(i 2kL)

• Here, only possible modification to the field is σ = |σ| e^(i φ)

• Term e^(i 2kL) naturally means that the phase of the field evolves during propagation

• Above defines an eigenvalue problem for E⃗ inside the cavity:

• (Generally infinite) discrete set of transverse modes: u_(a,b) ⇒ σ_(a,b)

• Fixing u_(a,b) = u we get a one specific transverse mode

• (Generally infinite and discrete) set of resonant frequencies (resulting from a phase shift of 2π n per round trip):

❴

ν_(a,b,n) = (c)/(2L) (n + φ_(a,b)2π )

φ_(a,b) + 2kL = 2π n

• Fixing ❴a,b❵: ν_(a,b,n) ~ ⇒ ~ νₙ , family of longitudinal modes

• Looking at the phase condition:

❴

ν_(a,b,n) = (c)/(2L) (n + φ_(ab)2π )

φ_(a,b) + 2kL = 2π n

• We see points worth noting:

• Cavity length defines the longitudinal mode spacing

• Transverse mode spacing is determined by symmetry

• For all n, an infinite number of transverse modes exist

• Question for discussions: for a given a and b, does an infinite number of longitudinal modes exist?

### 3.6 Photon Lifetime and Cavity Q-factor ###

## Cavity Photon Lifetime

• Next, we will study the temporal dynamics and properties of a laser cavity, that dictate e.g. the linewidth of the realized laser

• round-trip intensity: I = I₀ ρ₁ ρ₂ (1 - Lᵢ)² ~,~ Lᵢ=internal losses
Note that small reflections from optical components, such as from Brewster plates, also cause internal losses

• round-trip time: T_RT = (c)/(2L)

• Number of photons in the cavity ( φ ) is proportional to the intensity of the field inside the cavity

• The photon number φ follows the same functional form as the irradiance I :

φ = φ₀ ρ₁ ρ₂ (1 - Lᵢ)² (1 round-trip)

φ(m T_(RT)) = φ₀ [ ρ₁ ρ₂ (1 - Lᵢ)² ]ᵐ (m round-trips)

lim_(m → ∞) [ φ(m T_(RT)) ] = lim_(m → ∞) [ φ₀ e^(m ln(ρ₁ ρ₂ (1 - Lᵢ)²)) ] ∼ φ₀ e^(-t / τ_c) (m → ∞ limit)

• For an infinite multiple (m = ∞) of round-trips, we can calculate the photon number decay time of the cavity through the above expression

• Solving t=m T_(RT) for m, and substituting into above, we get

τ_c = (2L)/(c ln [ρ₁ ρ₂ (1 - Lᵢ)²]) (photon cavity lifetime)

• Decay time τ_c corresponds physically to the average time a photon spends in the cavity before leaving it

• Note: Since the number of photons φ in the cavity decays, so do the field intensity and the electric field

E(t) = E₀ e^(-t / τ_c) e^(-i ω t)

• Looking at above simple equation, we see that the Lorentzian frequency linewidth can be defined as the inverse of the cavity lifetime

Δ ω_c =(1)/(τ_c)

## Cavity Q-factor

• An important parameter to describe the temporal behavior of the resonator is known as the quality factor (Q-factor), and is defined via

Q = ω₀ Energy storedEnergy loss

• * Energy stored = # photons in the cavity ∼ φ = φ₀ e^(-t / τ_c)

• * Energy loss = # photons that leave the cavity ∼ dφ / dt

Q = ω₀ (φ)/(-(dφ)/(dt)) = (ω₀)/(Δ ω_c) ~ ,~ since

(dφ)/(dt) = φ₀ · (d)/(dt) (e^(-t / τ_c) ) = - (φ₀)/(τ_c) e^(-t / τ_c)
= - (φ)/(τ_c)
= - φ Δ ω_c

• Note that for the Q-factor following approximately applies

Q = (ω₀)/(Δ ω_c) ≈ ω₀ τ_c

• Quality factor is proportional to the cavity lifetime
⇒ Higher the mirror reflectivities ρₖ , the longer the photon stays in the cavity, the higher the Q -factor

• The longer a photon stays inside the cavity, the longer it can participate in the amplification process, and therefore contribute to the laser action

• Design tip: Cavity 'must' have the highest Q-factor possible

• Discussion question: How the Q-factor of the cavity affects the output power of the laser?

### 4.1 Rate Equations Model and Laser Parameters ###

• Next we want to understand how the laser resonator behaves dynamically, how to e.g.~estimate the lasing threshold condition, how to calculate output power, how laser could support short pulses of light, etc.

• Pulsed lasers emits (short) bursts of light, with a certain periodicity (repetition rate)
⇒ Lasers emitting ultrashort pulses are called ultrafast lasers^*
^*Operation of pulsed lasers, in all their glory, is treated in e.g.~FYS.502 Advanced Laser Technology

• Continuous-wave (CW) lasers emit light of time-independent intensity

• In our treatment, we only consider

the CW behaviour

• We consider the simplest model providing adequate physics, known as the space-independent model

• Assumptions we make:

• Pumping to the active medium is provided in some way (electrically or optically)

• The system is a 4-level system

• Cavity supports only one longitudinal mode (frequency ν₀)

• Transverse mode profile is constant at any location in the resonator (no beam divergence)

• Gain medium is homogeneously broadened

• Interactions in the active medium are independent of position

• In a 4-level system, state |1⟩
can always be treated as empty

• We can focus our attention
only on the state |2⟩

(dN₂)/(dt) = Wₚ N_g - W N₂ - (N₂)/(τ) , W = σ F ,~ F ∼ φ

• where

Wₚ = pump absorption rate

Nᵢ = population of the iᵗʰ state

τ = τ_(eff) = combined spontaneous emission and

= ~non-radiative decay time (1τ_(eff)=1τₛₚ + 1τₙᵣ)

## Laser Rate Equations

• We need to determine the photon number dynamics

W = B φ(t)

• The absorption rate is proportional to the number of photons in the cavity

• We assume a constant-rate pumping:

Wₚ N_g = Rₚ = constant

• We then write a rate equation for the number of photons as well:

❴

dN₂dt = Rₚ - B φ N₂ - N₂/τ , Population dynamics

dφdt = B φ N₂ Vₐ - φ/τ_c , Photon dynamics

• giving us the laser rate equations

• Physical meaning/significance of the terms:

B φ N₂ Vₐ = Increase of # photons due to stimulated emission

Vₐ = Mode volume in the active medium

= ~(volume of active medium occupied by the E-field)

φ / τ_c = Resonator losses (cavity lifetime)

• The laser rate equations above can be used to describe both continuous-wave (CW) and pulsed lasers

• However, we need to express the B coefficient, and τ_c as functions of the cavity parameters in order to have proper understanding

• Next we are going to derive expression for B and τ_c

• Note: We have already calculated τ_c, but for an empty/ideal cavity!

• We need to generalize τ_c for a complete resonator, consisting of mirrors (with reflection coefficients of ρᵢ), cavity of length L and losses of Lᵢ, hosting also a gain medium of length ℓ and gain g

• Evolution of intensity after one round-trip is

I = I₀ e^(g ℓ) (1 - Lᵢ) ρ₂ e^(gℓ) (1 - Lᵢ) ρ₁

g = σ (N₂ - N₁) ≈ σ N₂ (4-level approximation: N₁ ≈ 0)

• The evolution of intensity is then given by

Δ I = I - I₀ = I₀ [ e^(2g ℓ) (1 - Lᵢ)² ρ₁ ρ₂ - 1 ]

• Let's use logarithmic losses defined as

γ = - (1)/(2 ln [(1 - Lᵢ)² ρ₁ ρ₂ ])

• so that

Δ I = I₀ ( e^(2gℓ - 2γ) - 1 )

• For a good cavity, the losses must be small, so we can reasonably assume that γ ≪ 1

• In typical situations, the gain times the gain medium length is also very small: g ℓ ≪ 1

• We can then Taylor expand the exponential to obtain:

Δ I = I₀ ( e^(2(gℓ - γ)) - 1 ) ≈ 2I₀ (gℓ - γ)

• This implies:

Δ I ∼ Δ φ

• So the variation of # of photons in the cavity per cavity round trip can be written as

Δ φ = 2φ (σ N₂ ℓ - γ) ⇒ (Δ φ)/(Δ t) = (2φ (σ N₂ ℓ - γ))/(2Lₑ/c) ~, since~ Δ t = (2Lₑ)/(c)

• where

Lₑ = L + (n - 1)ℓ = Effective optical cavity length

• If we assume that (Δ φ)/(Δ t) holds at all times, we can switch to differentials, and write

(dφ)/(dt) = ( (σ N₂ ℓ c)/(Lₑ) ) φ - ( (γ c)/(Lₑ) ) φ

• Let's compare above with the rate equations we wrote earlier:

❴

d φdt = B N₂ Vₐ φ - φ/τ_c

d φdt = ( σ ℓ cLₑ N₂ - γ cLₑ ) φ

• From this, we can derive:

B = (σ ℓ c)/(Vₐ Lₑ) ≡ (σ c)/(V)

• where V = Vₐ Lₑℓ is the mode volume in the resonator

## B Coefficient and Cavity Lifetime

• The cavity lifetime is dictated by the properties of the cavity:

τ_c = (Lₑ)/(γ c ) = - (2Lₑ)/(c ln [(1 - Lᵢ²) ρ₁ ρ₂ ])

• We can then write the laser rate equations in their final form as:

(dN₂)/(dt) = Rₚ - B φ N₂ - (N₂)/(τ)

(dφ)/(dt) = B Vₐ N₂ φ - (φ)/(τ_c)

• where the parameters are defined as:

B = (σ c)/(V) and τ_c = (Lₑ)/(c γ) = - (2Lₑ)/(c ln [(1 - Lᵢ²) ρ₁ ρ₂ ])

### 4.2 CW Behaviour, Part I ###

## When Gain > Losses

• The laser rate equations are:

(dN₂)/(dt) = Rₚ - B φ N₂ - (N₂)/(τ)

(dφ)/(dt) = B Vₐ N₂ φ - (φ)/(τ_c)

• The important question to ask is, when the number of photons in the cavity increases?

• The number of photons φ increases when:

(dΦ)/(dt) > 0 ⇒ B Vₐ N₂(τ,Rₚ,φ,t) - (1)/(τ_c) > 0

• which is known as the amplification condition

• So, φ starts growing when the gain overcomes the losses

## Critical Inversion

• The amplification condition implies that the upper laser level must support a minimum population:

B Vₐ N₂ - (1)/(τ_c) > 0 ⇒ N₂ > N_c ⇒ N_c ≡ (1)/(B Vₐ τ_c) = (γ)/(σ ℓ)

• where N_c is known as the critical inversion

• The population of the upper laser level N₂ must be greater than N_c , for lasing action

to start happening

## Population Threshold

• What is the weakest pumping level required to achieve N_c?

• We seek for a steady-state solution:

(dN₂)/(dt) = 0 ⇒ Rₚ - (N_c)/(τ) = 0

• where the fact that we're looking at the threshold condition, implies N₂ = N_c, and that Bφ N₂ =0, since stimulated emission is still negligible

• We can define the critical pumping rate as

R_(cp) ≡ (N_c)/(τ) = (γ)/(σ ℓ τ)

• Decreases for lower losses γ

• Decreases for larger cross-section σ and gain length ℓ

• The pump threshold condition gives us guiding rules for optimizing pumping rate
⇒ Laser design should often aim to minimize γ and maximize σ and ℓ to achieve lasing with low pump threshold

• We now turn our attention to the photon number evolution inside the cavity

• Let's consider a case with pump below the critical value

N ≤ N_C ⇒ φ = 0

### 4.3 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation ###

• When talking about laser modes, and for example of single- vs. multi-mode operation, we refer to longitudinal modes

• A single-mode laser^* emits light at one longitudinal mode, in practise exhibiting an extremely narrow linewidth (from a few Hz to kHz, e.g. at 1550 nm Δ λ ≈ 10⁻⁶-10⁻⁹ nm)
^*Frequency-stabilized lasers operating at single longitudinal mode are called single-frequency lasers

• As the free-spectral range (FSR) is inversely proportional to cavity length, single-mode operation is easier when using short laser cavities

• Multi-mode lasers lase simultaneously at several modes, and depending the laser type, modes can either be phase-locked or oscillate independently of other modes

## Single-mode Operation

• Single-mode operation is important, as it affects the

• Spectral purity and coherence properties

• Precision and accuracy of laser-based measurements

• Enable absolute distance measurements (LIDARs), measurements of Doppler shifts (velocity/temperature), stable locking to atomic transitions (time keeping and metrology)

• Narrow linewidth (Hz--MHz) implies long coherence lengths (m--km), and also possibility for very precise measurements

Δ x ∝ Δ ν/ν or Δ t ∝ Δ ν/ν

enabling interferometric measurements and minimizing frequency noise

• Example applications include:

• Interferometry (e.g. metrology, gravitational sensing)

• Optical clocks

• Laser cooling and trapping

• Optical communications

• Nonlinear optics

• High-resolution spectroscopy

• Frequency combs (using single-mode laser as a driving laser)

## Multi-mode Operation

• Multi-mode operation is important, as it

• affects the efficiency of extracting all of available gain

• helps to realize high-power lasers

• reduces the coherence (useful in many applications)

• In particular, multi-mode operation is essential when using mode locking to make ultrafast lasers

• Ultrashort pulses are formed by temporally overlapping hundreds, or even thousands of longitudinal modes
⇒ Fixed phase relation between modes ⇒ ultrashort pulses

• Note that in this case the mode-locked laser operates in a highly-multimode way, but it still is temporally very coherent

• Example applications include:

• Laser printing

• Materials processing (high powers)

• Illumination (no speckle formation)

• Optical pumping (more stable and more homogeneous pumping)

• LIDARs with reduced speckle formation

• Nonlinear optics (ultrafast lasers with high peak-intensities)

## Spectral Hole Burning

• Next, we will introduce two mechanisms that strongly favor multi-mode operation in lasers

• Mechanisms require inhomogeneously broadened gain media

• Key concept: Atoms in the gain medium exhibit different resonance frequencies, caused by

• Doppler broadening, crystal-field variations, carrier distributions

⇒ Broad, often Gaussian, gain profile spanning Δν_g ∼ 100 GHz

• When pump rate exceeds lasing threshold, only resonator modes near the gain peak can reach the lasing condition (gain = losses)
⇒ Only resonant atoms are saturated by stimulated emission

• Why saturation varies at different frequencies?

• Atoms at frequency ν₀ are saturated by the lasing field at ν₀

• Atoms at frequencies ν₁, ν₂ (away from ν₀) remain unsaturated, because stimulated emission at ν₀ doesn't affect atoms tuned to ν₁ or ν₂

• Therefore, different spectral regions of the gain profile saturate independently

• When pump power increases further, adjacent longitudinal modes (at frequencies ν₁, ν₂) can now independently reach the lasing threshold
⇒ results in spectral hole burning and multimode oscillation

• Let's consider the ideal case of homogeneous broadening

• All atoms contribute equally to a uniform (Lorentzian) gain

• Once a mode reaches threshold, the gain “clamps” at the threshold value for all modes

• This ideally favors single-mode operation, since no other modes can independently satisfy gain = losses

• For homogeneously broadened active media:

• ⇒ saturation of gain elements clamps the whole gain

• ⇒ single-mode operation persists

• ⇒ good coherence properties persist at higher lasing powers

## Spatial Hole Burning

• Let's finally consider spatial hole burning, as an example process complicating behavior of lasers (complex systems)

• In standing-wave (linear) resonators, the intracavity lasing field forms stationary intensity patterns with nodes and antinodes along the longitudinal direction

• Atoms at antinodes are saturated by lasing action of one longitudinal mode, while atoms in the nodes exhibit gain

• Adjacent longitudinal modes, whose antinodes align with the previous mode’s nodes, can use this unsaturated gain, enabling multiple modes to oscillate even in a homogeneously broadened medium

• Spatial hole burning can happen in linear resonators, where pump beam forms standing waves:

• ⇒ spatially uneven saturation

• ⇒ spatial hole burning

• ⇒ multimode oscillation and reduced coherence

• Effect occurs even in homogeneously broadened media

• So, laser development is often a holistic process, where many effects affect each other, and the behavior of the laser

• While the oscillation of multiple longitudinal modes is often extremely detrimental, that is not always the case (check out what mode-locked lasers are)

### 4.4 Single-Mode Selection ###

• As we discovered in the previous chapter, complex systems, such as lasers, can support existence of multiple modes

• In addition to existence of multiple modes, mode coupling and subsequent complex dynamics can considerably complicate their behavior
⇒ Multi-mode, or even chaotic, operation is often very detrimental for performance

• As an example, lasers naturally oscillate at multiple longitudinal modes

• For applications requiring very precise measurements, single-mode operation is often required, or highly preferential

## Short Cavities

• One approach for single-mode operation, is to design laser resonators that allow only a single longitudinal mode to lase

• This is realized by adjusting the spacing between longitudinal modes, i.e. the free spectral range (FSR) (Δ ν_(modes)) to be greater than the gain bandwidth (Δ ν_g/2)

• This condition can be expressed as:

Δ ν_(modes) > Δ ν_g/2 , where Δ ν_(modes) = c/2L_(eff)

• Rearranging gives a constraint on the effective cavity length:

L_(eff) < c / Δν_g

• For typical gain bandwidths (Δ ν_g ≈ 100~GHz), this implies L_(eff) < 3~mm, which can be challenging/limiting in practice

• A practical solution for single-mode operation is to insert a frequency-selective filter into the cavity

• The filter introduces frequency-dependent losses:

• Low losses near the operation frequency

• High losses elsewhere

• This ensures only one longitudinal mode meets the lasing threshold condition (gain = losses)

• Adjacent modes experience high losses and cannot reach threshold, enforcing single-mode operation

## Spectral Frequency Filtering

• One possibility to implement a filter with frequency-dependent losses, is to use a Fabry-Perot (FP) etalon/interferometer in the cavity

• However, since the transmission (T) function of a FP interferometer is a periodic function of frequency, the free-spectral range (FSR) of the FP must be larger than half of the FWHM of the gain curve

Δ ν^(FP)_(FSR) ≥ (Δ ν_g)/(2)

• This condition is easily met with etalons, that are relatively thin components (millimeter thickness)

• In addition, the below condition on the longitudinal mode spacing (given by the laser cavity) must hold

Δ ν_(modes) ≥ Δ ν_(FP)2 where Δ ν_(FP) = FWHM of the filter's peak

• Using the above two conditions, we can write

Δ ν_(modes) ≥ Δ ν_(FP)2Δ ν^(FP)_(FSR) Δ ν^(FP)_(FSR) ≥ Δ ν^(FP)_(FSR)2F_(FP) ≥ Δ ν_g 4F_(FP)

• showing how increasing the finesse of the etalon helps establishing single-mode

operation

• Since the longitudinal-mode spacing is dependent on the cavity length, Δ ν_(modes) = c/ L_(eff), the above condition can be also formulated in terms of the overall (effective) length of the laser cavity

Lₑ ≤ (2cF)/(Δ ν_g)

• With a typical value F = 30 for the finesse of a FP resonator, and a gain bandwidth (FWHM) Δ ν₀ = 100 GHz, the (effective) resonator length must be now smaller than 18 cm^*
^*Such short cavities are easily realized using vertical external-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VECSELs)
⇒ strongly relaxed constraint compared to the non-filtered resonator!

## Spatial Mode Filtering

• Above, we only discussed how a single longitudinal mode is made to oscillate, and thus participate to the lasing action

• It is often useful to force a single transverse mode to oscillate (affects e.g.~beam divergence)

• Often the ideal configuration is that only the fundamental Gaussian beam propagates inside the cavity

• The easiest solution to above is to exploit the cylindrical symmetry of a Gaussian beam, and insert an aperture of radius R (whose value is as close as possible to the beam waist of the Gaussian beam) somewhere in the cavity

• This aperture act as a spatial filter, whose transmission function is different for the different modes in the cavity^*
^*Note the analog between frequency filtering to realize single longitudinal-mode operation

• E.g. in case of a circular aperture, it will introduce small losses for the fundamental Gaussian beam (because of cylindrical symmetry), while it will introduce higher losses to the higher-order Hermite-Gaussian modes of the cavity, since their symmetry does not match the one of the aperture

• One should be careful when choosing the radius of the aperture, wrongly sized aperture, along with the losses introduced by it, may drive the cavity into instability

Continuous Wave Behaviour of a Laser
In this chapter, we combine the features of active medium and optical resonator studied in
earlier chapters, to finally describe how a laser works [see Fig. 4.1]. To make things simpler,
we limit ourselves to study the so-called continuous wave (CW) behaviour of a laser, i.e.,
we analyse what happens to the laser cavity and the emitted laser radiation, once thermal
equilibrium has been reached in the cavity, and the emitted intensity does not depend on time
anymore. Throughout this chapter, we assume that the pumping to the gain medium, necessary
for it to reach the population inversion condition, is provided in some form, either optically or
electrically. We also consider the active medium to be a 4-level system, as population inversion
can be realised much easier, than in a 3-level system.
Furthermore, we make a series of assumptions, that in principle are not required for guaranteeing the proper functioning of a laser, but they are casted here, in order to simplify the
treatment, and to focus only on the salient features of the laser cavity. These assumptions are
the following:
• only a single mode propagates inside the resonator. The mode is characterised by a
frequency ⌫0;
• the mode profile is constant at any location in the resonator;
• the active medium is assumed to be homogeneously broadened, with a Lorentzian lineshape;
• the interactions in the active medium do not depend on the location inside the medium
itself.

framework, we can use the rate equations to study the properties of the laser. Rate equations,
