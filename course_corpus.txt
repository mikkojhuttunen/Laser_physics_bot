𝟙.𝟚 The Laser Idea
➤ Laser operation is in principle simple and can be divided into
three steps
1) Achieve light amplification in the active medium via
stimulated emission
2) Introduce a feedback mechanism to sustain
amplification (i.e. oscillation)
- Achieved using an optical cavity
- The optical cavity also introduces losses
3) Supply energy to the medium via a pumping mechanism
- Needed to exceed lasing threshold and start the lasing
➤ Next we will briefly explain these functions and governing
main principles
15

𝟙.𝟛 Pumping Schemes - 2-Level System
➤ Population inversion cannot be achieved in a two-level

● Even with continuous pumping, the system can only reach an equilibrium,
but not inversion
➤ Therefore, two-level systems cannot support laser operation
➤ To enable population inversion and laser action, one must use
a more complex system:
● Three-level or four-level atomic/molecular systems are typically

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation
➤ When talking about laser modes, and for example of singlevs. multi-mode operation, we refer to longitudinal modes
➤ A single-mode laser∗ emits light at one longitudinal mode,
in practise exhibiting an extremely narrow linewidth (from
a few Hz to kHz, e.g. at 1550 nm Δλ ≈ 10–9 – 10–12 pm)
➤ As the free-spectral range (FSR) is inversely proportional to
cavity length, single-mode operation is easier when using short
laser cavities
➤ Multi-mode lasers simultaneously lase at several modes,
and depending the laser type, modes can either be
phase-locked or oscillate independently of other modes
∗Frequency-stabilized lasers operating at single longitudinal mode are called single-frequency lasers
200

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Single-mode Operation
➤ Single-mode operation is important, as it affects the
● Spectral purity and coherence properties
● Precision and accuracy of laser-based measurements
➤ Enable absolute distance measurements (LIDARs),
measurements of Doppler shifts (velocity/temperature), stable
locking to atomic transitions (time keeping and metrology)
➤ Narrow linewidth (Hz–MHz) implies long coherence lengths
(m–km), and also possibility for very precise measurements
Δx ∝ Δν/ν or Δt ∝ Δν/ν
enabling interferometric measurements and minimizing
frequency noise
201

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Single-mode Operation
➤ Example applications include:
● Interferometry (e.g. metrology, gravitational sensing)
● Optical clocks
● Laser cooling and trapping
● Optical communications
● Nonlinear optics
● High-resolution spectroscopy
● Frequency combs (using single-mode laser as a driving laser)
202

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Multi-mode Operation
➤ Multi-mode operation is important, as it
● affects the efficiency of extracting all of available gain
● helps to realize high-power lasers
● reduces the coherence (useful in many applications)
➤ In particular, multi-mode operation is essential when using
mode locking to make ultrafast lasers
➤ Ultrashort pulses are formed by temporally overlapping
hundreds, or even thousands of longitudinal modes
=> Fixed phase relation between modes ⇒ ultrashort pulses
➤ Note that in this case the mode-locked laser operates in a
highly-multimode way, but it still is temporally very coherent
203

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Multi-mode Operation
➤ Example applications include:
● Laser printing
● Materials processing (high powers)
● Illumination (no speckle formation)
● Optical pumping (more stable and more homogeneous pumping)
● LIDARs with reduced speckle formation
● Nonlinear optics (ultrafast lasers with high peak-intensities)

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Spectral Hole Burning
➤ Next, we will introduce two mechanisms that strongly favor
multi-mode operation in lasers
➤ Mechanisms require inhomogeneously broadened gain media
➤ Atoms in the gain medium then exhibit a distribution
of resonance frequencies (e.g. Doppler broadening,
crystal-field variations, carrier distributions)—forming a
broad, and typically Gaussian, gain profile
➤ Now as the pump rate exceeds lasing threshold, only the laser
resonator modes near the gain peak initially reach the lasing
condition (gain = losses)
=> only atoms resonant with those modes can saturate
205

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Spectral Hole Burning
➤ Other resonant active∗ atom subsets remain unsaturated, so
when pump power is further increased, adjacent (longitudinal)
modes can now independently reach the lasing threshold
=> results in spectral hole burning and multimode oscillation
➤ Process for spectral hole burning:
● We consider an inhomogeneously broadened active medium
⇒ independent saturation of gain elements
⇒ multiple modes can start oscillating
⇒ multi-mode operation reduces coherence
∗Note on terminology: active medium = gain medium, active elements = gain elements etc.
206

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Homogeneous Broadening
➤ Let’s consider the ideal case of homogeneous broadening
➤ All atoms contribute equally to a uniform (Lorentzian) gain
➤ Once a mode reaches threshold, the gain “clamps” at the
threshold value for all modes
➤ This ideally favors single-mode operation, since no other
modes can independently satisfy gain = losses
➤ For homogeneously broadened active media:
⇒ saturation of gain elements clamps the whole gain
⇒ single-mode operation persists
⇒ Good coherence properties persist at higher lasing powers
207

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Spatial Hole Burning
➤ Let’s finally consider spatial hole burning, as an example
process complicating behavior of lasers (complex systems)
➤ In standing-wave (linear) resonators, the intracavity field
forms stationary intensity patterns with nodes and
antinodes along the longitudinal direction
➤ Atoms at antinodes are saturated by lasing action of one
longitudinal mode, while atoms in the nodes exhibit gain
➤ Adjacent longitudinal modes—whose antinodes align with
the previous mode’s nodes—can use this unsaturated gain,
enabling multiple modes to oscillate even in a
homogeneously broadened medium
208

𝟜.𝟛 Reasons for Multimode Oscillation - Spatial Hole Burning
➤ Spatial hole burning can happen in linear resonators, where
pump beam forms standing waves:
⇒ spatially uneven saturation
⇒ spatial hole burning
⇒ multimode oscillation and reduced coherence
➤ So, laser development is often a holistic process, where many
effects affect each other, and the behavior of the laser
➤ While the oscillation of multiple longitudinal modes is seen
detrimental here, that is not always the case (check out
what mode-locked lasers are)

the coherence time ⌧c is finite, and related to the non-perfect monochromaticity of lasers.
1.4.3 Directionality
The directionality property of a laser beam derives directly from the fact, that the laser active
medium is placed into an optical cavity. If such cavity consists of only two mirrors, for example,
only the waves propagating in a direction orthogonal to the mirrors (and near the optical
axis of the cavity itself) will be sustained over many round-trips. As a result, the radiation
pattern that leaves the cavity, i.e., the laser beam, will propagate mainly along one particular
direction (opposed, for example, to spherical waves, that do not have a well-defined propagation
direction), exhibiting very small di↵raction (i.e., the transverse beam size will grow very slowly
along the propagation direction).

Continuous Wave Behaviour of a Laser
In this chapter, we combine the features of active medium and optical resonator studied in
earlier chapters, to finally describe how a laser works [see Fig. 4.1]. To make things simpler,
we limit ourselves to study the so-called continuous wave (CW) behaviour of a laser, i.e.,
we analyse what happens to the laser cavity and the emitted laser radiation, once thermal
equilibrium has been reached in the cavity, and the emitted intensity does not depend on time
anymore. Throughout this chapter, we assume that the pumping to the gain medium, necessary
for it to reach the population inversion condition, is provided in some form, either optically or
electrically. We also consider the active medium to be a 4-level system, as population inversion
can be realised much easier, than in a 3-level system.
Furthermore, we make a series of assumptions, that in principle are not required for guaranteeing the proper functioning of a laser, but they are casted here, in order to simplify the
treatment, and to focus only on the salient features of the laser cavity. These assumptions are
the following:
• only a single mode propagates inside the resonator. The mode is characterised by a
frequency ⌫0;
• the mode profile is constant at any location in the resonator;
• the active medium is assumed to be homogeneously broadened, with a Lorentzian lineshape;
• the interactions in the active medium do not depend on the location inside the medium
itself.
